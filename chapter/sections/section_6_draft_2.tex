% - burden-sharing: every country should participate, burden should be equitably shared

International redistributive mechanisms, including some climate-policy schemes, must determine how countries share the costs. Public acceptance is likely to be higher when a broad coalition participates and contributions reflect each country's capacity.

Survey experiments on climate burden-sharing confirm the importance of institutional design. Support increases when many countries participate and share the costs rather than leaving most of the burden to the respondent's country. Respondents nevertheless remain willing to accept substantial domestic contributions. \citet{bechtel_mass_2013} conducted survey experiments in 2012 with nationally representative samples from France, Germany, the United Kingdom, and the United States. Each respondent completed four comparisons between two randomly generated international climate agreements. For each comparison, respondents selected their preferred agreement and reported how likely they were to vote for or against their country's participation in a referendum. % AF: ajouter "how likely" l'électeur médian est de voter pour l'accord préféré et le pire accord (ou plutôt la proportion d'électeurs qui voteraient oui)
The agreements varied in their monthly household cost, the distribution of costs across countries, the number of participating countries, the share of global emissions covered, the monitoring institution, and the sanctions for non-compliance. These design features strongly affected support. Increasing costs from 0.5\% to 1\% of national GDP reduced the probability of selecting an agreement by approximately 10 percentage points. By contrast, increasing participation from 20 to 160 countries out of 192 raised it by about 15 points \citep[Table 1 \& Figure 2]{bechtel_mass_2013}.

Focusing on North--South climate finance, \citet{gampfer_obtaining_2014} conducted survey experiments with nationally representative samples in the United States and Germany between October and November 2013. Respondents considered six pairs of hypothetical climate-finance mechanisms and indicated, for each pair, ``which of the two proposals they preferred''. The mechanisms varied in recipient countries' climate damages, income, emissions, and government efficiency; the actors controlling allocation decisions; the use of funds for mitigation, adaptation, or both; and the distribution of contributions between the respondent's country and other donor countries. Donor burden-sharing produced by far the largest effect. Relative to a mechanism in which other countries provided 10\% of the funds and the respondent's country provided 90\%, reversing these shares increased the probability of selection by 28 %27.9 
percentage points in the United States and 23 %23.4 
points in Germany. Respondents nevertheless accepted substantial domestic contributions: most of the increase in support occurred when the share paid by other countries rose from 10\% to 30\% or 50\%. 
Joint allocation by donor and recipient countries increased the probability of selection by 6 percentage points in the United States and 9 points in Germany, relative to allocation exclusively by recipient countries. % AF: je comprends pas ce que ça veut dire
Respondents also preferred mechanisms that financed both mitigation and adaptation to those that financed adaptation alone \citep[Table 1 \& Figure 2]{gampfer_obtaining_2014}.

\citet{fabre_public_2026} examines public acceptance of a Global Climate Scheme (GCS), presented to respondents as a mechanism designed to meet the 2\textdegree{} climate target. The scheme would cap emissions by issuing a limited number of tradable permits, which polluting firms would have to purchase to cover their greenhouse-gas emissions. The resulting increase in fossil-fuel prices would discourage consumption and reduce emissions. In accordance with the principle that every individual has an equal right to emit, the revenues from permit sales would finance a global basic income. Each adult would receive an average of \$35 per month, thereby lifting around 600 million people who live on less than \$2 per day out of extreme poverty. The typical resident would incur a net monthly loss of \$90 in the United States while receiving a \$35 transfer, compared with a loss of \texteuro 15 and a transfer of \texteuro 20 in France, \texteuro 45 and \texteuro 20 in Germany, CHF 15 and CHF 20 in Switzerland, and RUB 2,500 and RUB 3,000 in Russia. In the United States, the estimated net loss of \$90 per month reflects a 2\% increase in consumer prices after accounting for the \$35 transfer \citep[Table S2]{fabre_public_2026}.

The International Climate Scheme (ICS) applies the same cap-and-trade mechanism as the GCS, but within different coalition in the world. Respondents are randomly assigned to one of four variants and shown a map identifying the participating countries and their share of global emissions. In the \textit{Low} variant, the coalition comprises the Global South and the European Union, but excludes China, covering 33\% of global emissions. The \textit{Mid} variant replaces the EU with China and brings together China and the Global South, which account for 56\% of global emissions. The \textit{High} variant adds the EU, the United Kingdom, Japan, Canada, South Korea, and other high-income countries to the \textit{Mid} coalition, raising coverage to 72\% of global emissions. Finally, the \textit{High color} variant retains the same coalition as the \textit{High} variant but uses a colored map to display the net financial gain or cost of the scheme for each country, with middle-income countries such as China protected from financial losses \citep[Figure 5]{fabre_public_2026}. Wider participation increases acceptance only modestly: support rises from 65\% in the \textit{Low} variant to 68\% in the \textit{High} variant. By contrast, explicitly displaying national gains and losses reduces support by 6.6 percentage points relative to the \textit{High} variant, suggesting that the salience of distributive consequences matters more than the precise size of the coalition \citep[Figure 6]{fabre_public_2026}.

The International Climate Scheme (ICS) applies the GCS cap-and-trade mechanism to coalitions with different country coverage. The survey randomly assigns respondents to one of four variants and shows them a map of the participating countries and their share of global emissions. In the \textit{Low} variant, the Global South and the European Union participate, while China does not. Depending on the respondent's country, this coalition covers 25--33\% of global emissions. The \textit{Mid} variant replaces the EU with China. China and the Global South jointly account for 56\% of global emissions. The \textit{High} variant adds the EU, the United Kingdom, Japan, Canada, South Korea, and other high-income countries to the \textit{Mid} coalition, raising its coverage to 64--72\% of global emissions. Finally, the \textit{High color} variant retains the same coalition but uses colors to indicate each country's net financial gain or loss, while protecting middle-income countries such as China from financial losses \citep[Figure 5]{fabre_public_2026}. Country coverage has only a modest effect on acceptance. Support reaches 65\% in the \textit{Low} variant and 68\% in the \textit{High} variant, an estimated difference of 3.8 percentage points. By contrast, the \textit{High color} variant reduces support by 6.6 percentage points relative to the \textit{High} variant. This larger decline suggests that making cross-country distributive consequences salient may matter more than the precise size of the coalition, although the experiment does not identify a single mechanism \citep[Figure 6]{fabre_public_2026}.

\citet{douenne_yellow_2022} surveyed a representative sample of French households in February and March 2019, shortly after the government withdrew a planned increase in the carbon tax. The authors presented respondents with a tax-and-dividend reform that would increase the French carbon tax by \texteuro 50/tCO$_2$, raise transport and energy prices, and return the revenues through an annual lump-sum payment of \texteuro 110 to each adult. They then asked: ``Would you approve of this reform?'', offering ``Yes'', ``No'', and ``I don't know / prefer not to respond'' as possible answers. Only 10\% of respondents approved the reform, while 70\% opposed it. Although the reform would leave approximately 70\% of households objectively better off, only 14\% expected to gain from it. Most respondents also regarded the scheme as regressive and environmentally ineffective. Believing that one's household would avoid a loss predicts 53.4 percentage points higher acceptance. Similarly, believing that the reform would effectively reduce pollution and climate change predicts 41.6 percentage points higher approval and 50.5 points higher acceptance \citep[Tables 6 \& 8]{douenne_yellow_2022}. The authors also use perceived tax progressivity as a proxy for fairness. Perceived progressivity predicts 27 percentage points higher acceptance, although unobserved confounding may affect this estimate.
